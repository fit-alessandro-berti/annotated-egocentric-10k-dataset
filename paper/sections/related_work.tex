\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}
\subsection{Unstructured observations as process data}
K\"onig et al.~\cite{konig2025unstructured} review how unstructured data enters process mining, including the construction of logs from text, images, and video. Diba et al.~\cite{diba2020extraction} distinguish extraction, correlation, and abstraction. These tasks require decisions about which observations become events, how events are grouped, and which concepts describe them. Andrews et al.~\cite{andrews2020quality} make data quality part of construction in RDB2Log, which helps analysts derive logs from relational data. Our setting adds a chain of inferred descriptions and labels before a log exists. We examine how choices along that chain affect the resulting measurements.

\subsection{Video and first-person workflow understanding}
Petersen et al.~\cite{petersen2013workflows} model and track manual workflows from first-person video to support augmented-reality instructions and feedback during task execution. Pham and Xiao~\cite{pham2018workflow} extract assembly steps and operations with semantic descriptions, without pre-labeled training data or pre-trained classifiers. These studies establish first-person workflow understanding as an antecedent. Their goals include following a demonstrated task and recovering its steps.

Our objective is retrospective process analysis across recorded workers. We construct event data to describe time composition, changes between operational categories, and variation in recorded work. A shared vocabulary lets these descriptions use the same names within a factory. It does not establish that workers performed equivalent tasks or that a category transition was a physical interruption. Egocentric video provides access to the wearer's work; the analytical objective and event construction define its role here.

Video process mining also has several established designs. Knoch et al.~\cite{knoch2020video} use hand trajectories and clustering at a camera-equipped assembly workstation. Kratsch et al.~\cite{kratsch2022video} develop a reference architecture, and Lepsien et al.~\cite{lepsien2023analytics} combine object tracking, action detection, and abstraction. SnapLog~\cite{pegoraro2026snaplog} uses pretrained visual representations, temporal segmentation, and a classifier adapted with labeled examples. Its evaluation includes TUM Kitchen and egocentric EPIC-KITCHENS recordings. Thus, camera placement and supervision must be compared for individual methods. The sensor-data review by Brzychczy et al.~\cite{brzychczy2025sensor} places these efforts within the broader problem of converting observations into process events.

\subsubsection{From video descriptions to process events}
Dense video captioning already combines temporal localization with natural-language descriptions~\cite{krishna2017densecaptioning}. Timestamped narration alone is therefore an established output. Process analysis requires further choices: reusable activity names across recordings, event granularity, selection of operationally relevant episodes, observational case identifiers, source links, and rules for interpreting durations and frequencies.

S1's instruction to merge stable repetition illustrates why these choices matter. Several repeated actions can become one narrated interval and later one event, so event frequency need not equal action frequency. Conversely, the existing foundry example in Section~\ref{sec:worked} turns one 31-second description into two events with shared factory labels and an inferred internal boundary. The added structure enables calculations, while changing what each row and duration represents.

\subsection{Video-to-log and language-model-based construction}
\label{sec:closest}
Gavric et al.'s Vid2log~\cite{gavric2024multimodal} is a close predecessor. It uses LLaVA to infer a domain and relevant activities from selected frames, obtains textual event information, and combines video-derived data with conventional logs through temporal and semantic matching. Both methods use natural language as an interface between visual observations and process event data. Our design first narrates recordings, aggregates descriptions by worker, and induces shared factory label lists before assigning events from the original transcripts. This ordering makes the available factory corpus part of the representation used to analyze it. Vid2log studies log richness and an assembly use case; our assessment examines transcript consistency and the consequences of event and category choices for duration and sequence analyses. The comparison centers on how factory context is constructed and how the resulting event representation is assessed.

Kecht et al.~\cite{kecht2021conversations} use pretrained natural language inference to assign supplied topics and activities to customer-service messages. They do not train a new language model, but use labeled messages to select classification thresholds and refine hypotheses. Message identifiers and timestamps are retained. Our method instead estimates episode boundaries from generated video descriptions and induces label lists from the factory material. Shirali et al.~\cite{shirali2025iot} also apply LLMs to physical observations: they abstract IoT readings into high-level events and integrate logs from multiple sensor sources. LLM use for physical-process data therefore predates this study. Work on process-mining assistants~\cite{berti2025benchmark} and database-query generation~\cite{dani2025llm} addresses neighboring tasks with different inputs and outputs.

Seeth et al.~\cite{seeth2026finetuned}, an arXiv preprint, study fine-tuning for text-to-log generation. They pair generated reports with source traces, supply permitted activities and attributes, and evaluate both traces and discovered process models against source-log references. They report benefits from fine-tuning over zero-shot and few-shot prompting in that setting. Our models receive no task-specific parameter updates; adaptation takes place through staged context and a vocabulary induced from each factory corpus. A permitted label list is shared by both designs and is not itself the distinction. The studies differ in source material, reference data, and evaluation targets. Our assessment establishes what the reported procedure supports, not that fine-tuning is generally unnecessary. Numerical scores from these studies cannot be ranked as equivalent measures of extraction accuracy.

\subsection{Event abstraction and semantic consistency}
The review by van Zelst et al.~\cite{vanZelst2021abstraction} explains how event abstraction changes process granularity and distinguishes methods by matters including supervision, input, and uncertainty. Here, three questions need separate treatment: where an episode begins and ends, which reusable activity classes exist, and which class is assigned to each episode. S1 mainly supplies candidate intervals, S2 and S3 construct the classes, and S4 assigns events and can revise boundaries. Section~\ref{sec:abstraction} maps these tasks to the implementation.

Inducing classes and assigning instances can fail in different ways. A useful concept can be missing from the vocabulary, or an available concept can be assigned incorrectly. A later category map can also hide a correctly recorded label. The activity and process fields are two label dimensions; the implementation does not establish a parent-child hierarchy. This distinction is necessary to explain factory \textbf{003}, where transport activities occur under process contexts that are mapped to other operational categories.

Text2EL+~\cite{kapugamageeganage2024text2el} enriches existing logs with events and attributes extracted from text, using semantic and contextual validation and expert guidance. Sturm et al.~\cite{sturm2026multimodal} instantiate parsing, abstraction, extraction, integration, and enrichment using CRM data, email metadata, and call transcripts. Their evaluation involves a sales process and a domain expert. Table~\ref{tab:construction_tasks} separates the tasks these designs can combine.

Our main task is construction. S1 to S4 derive an initial representation; S5 consolidates it using source filenames, factory and worker identifiers, and a stated ordering rule (Section~\ref{sec:cases}). It does not resolve product correspondences between independently recorded systems. Videos, narrations, and reports are successive representations of the same originating observations. Agreement among them does not provide the independent corroboration that separately recorded enterprise or sensor sources could supply.

\subsection{Quality, uncertainty, and relational representations}
Suriadi et al.~\cite{suriadi2017imperfections} organize recurring event-log imperfections. Our contribution to this concern is an account of how narration, vocabulary construction, assignment, and consolidation can introduce or propagate them. Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} connects each transformation to an existing check or example and its measurement consequence.

Quality annotations and explicitly uncertain events serve different purposes. Goel et al.~\cite{goel2022quality} retain data-quality information for use during analysis. Pegoraro et al.~\cite{pegoraro2021uncertain} represent uncertainty explicitly and compute conformance bounds. SnapLog can retain candidate-label distributions~\cite{pegoraro2026snaplog}. Our events instead have single-valued activity and process assignments. The audit records particular disagreements, and the five-second boundary tolerance is an evaluation rule. Neither provides calibrated event probabilities or bounds on total downstream error. Structured quality flags and explicit alternatives are possible extensions, with different evidence requirements.

Esser and Fahland~\cite{esser2021multidimensional} represent events and entities across multiple dimensions in graph databases. This complements the case-choice concerns of object-centric process mining~\cite{aalst2019objectcentric}. Our worker and video relationships support alternative observational views. Product identities and cross-worker product trajectories require additional evidence; a change in storage format cannot establish them. Similarly, the range of industrial process-mining goals~\cite{corallo2020industrial} makes it necessary to distinguish descriptions of recorded work from conclusions about production performance.

The question addressed here is how a shared representation, built from the available factory recordings before event assignment, supports retrospective analysis and shapes its results. \textbf{RQ1} examines that construction and its structural properties. \textbf{RQ2} checks the transcript-to-event transformation. \textbf{RQ3} studies the supported calculations, and \textbf{RQ4} explains their dependence on vocabulary, boundaries, case rules, and analytical categories. Together, these questions connect the construction design, artifact release, and measurement findings to their methodological antecedents.
