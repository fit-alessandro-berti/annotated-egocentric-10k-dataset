\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}
\subsection{Constructing and assessing event data}
Diba et al.~\cite{diba2020extraction} distinguish extraction, correlation, and abstraction when constructing event data. These tasks also arise here: video must become a description, episodes need labels, and a case rule must group them. The abstraction review by van Zelst et al.~\cite{vanZelst2021abstraction} explains how grouping low-level events changes the level at which a process can be studied. In our setting, a repeated sequence can already be compressed during narration, before a structured event exists. Consequently, a row count cannot be assumed to count cycles.

Suriadi et al.~\cite{suriadi2017imperfections} describe recurring event-log imperfections and their implications for analysis. Their work motivates checks for missing fields, overlapping intervals, and inconsistent time representations. These checks expose data problems but do not establish that a visually inferred activity is correct. We therefore separate structural checks, vocabulary checks, transcript consistency, and accuracy against footage.

\subsection{Video and sensor methods}
First-person workflow extraction without task-specific labeled training data predates this study. Pham and Xiao~\cite{pham2018workflow} extract mechanical-assembly workflows from first-person video without pre-labeled training data or pre-trained classifiers. Their preliminary evaluation already addresses the combination of egocentric observation and unsupervised extraction. Knoch et al.~\cite{knoch2020video} use hand trajectories and clustering at a camera-equipped assembly workstation, evaluated with ten participants. Kratsch et al.~\cite{kratsch2022video} develop a reference architecture for video-based process mining. Lepsien et al.~\cite{lepsien2023analytics} combine object tracking, action detection, and abstraction to produce event data. The sensor-data review by Brzychczy et al.~\cite{brzychczy2025sensor} addresses the related problem of turning lower-level signals into process events. These streams inform our separation of observation, labeling, and case construction.

SnapLog~\cite{pegoraro2026snaplog} combines image embeddings, temporal segmentation, and a classifier adapted with labeled examples. It evaluates both TUM Kitchen recordings and 24 egocentric EPIC-KITCHENS videos from ten environments. It can retain distributions over candidate activity labels. Thus, prior work is not uniformly restricted to fixed cameras, one workstation, or one supervision regime. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} makes the closest distinctions explicit. The present contribution is the combination of a multi-factory application, induced factory vocabularies, inspectable text, a layered artifact release, and an account of operational measurement. No comparative accuracy advantage is claimed.

\subsection{Language models, uncertain data, and manufacturing interpretation}
LLMs have been studied as assistants for process mining~\cite{berti2025benchmark} and for generating database queries used in event-log extraction~\cite{dani2025llm}. Kecht et al.~\cite{kecht2021conversations} address log construction from customer-service conversations using natural language inference. Here, the text is itself model-generated evidence. A semantic check against that text tests one link in the extraction chain, not the entire path from physical work to an analytical conclusion. This dependency is central to our assessment.

Work on uncertain event data makes uncertain labels, times, or event occurrence explicit~\cite{pegoraro2019uncertain}. Our deterministic CSVs do not implement such an uncertainty model. Readable intermediate text helps a reader investigate an event, but it does not provide calibrated probabilities or formal bounds. Similarly, object-centric process mining~\cite{aalst2019objectcentric} clarifies why worker and recording identifiers cannot substitute for product identifiers when tracing material across stations. These distinctions limit claims about throughput, yield, handoffs, and complete manufacturing instances.

Industrial process-mining research also spans several analytical goals, as reviewed by Corallo et al.~\cite{corallo2020industrial}. This motivates separating a description of recorded work from a decision about changing production. Our time-composition and sequence measures provide evidence for the former; the latter requires product, quality, and operational context absent from the inferred logs.
